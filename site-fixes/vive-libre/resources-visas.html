How long can you stay? Visas, explained.

The 90/180 rule, the non-lucrative visa and the digital nomad visa, in plain English for non-EU buyers.

The 90/180 rule

Without a visa, non-EU visitors (including UK citizens since Brexit) can spend up to 90 days in any rolling 180-day period across the Schengen area. Owning a home in Spain doesn't change that, as it isn't a right to stay.

The non-lucrative visa

For people who want to live in Spain without working here, often retirees or those living on pension or investment income. You'll need to show sufficient income or savings, private health insurance and a clean criminal record. It doesn't allow you to work in Spain.

The digital nomad visa

For remote workers employed by, or freelancing for, companies outside Spain. You'll need proof of income, relevant qualifications or experience, and evidence that the work can be done remotely.

EU citizens

With an EU passport you can live here freely. Stays of more than three months need registering, and you'll be asked to show income or savings and health cover.

What about the golden visa?

Spain closed the property-investment golden visa route in 2025, so buying a home no longer leads to residency on its own.

Settling in: the practical essentials

Once your residency route is clear, these are the things that make day-to-day life work.

NIE number

Your NIE (número de identidad de extranjero) is the identification number you need to buy property, open a bank account, sign utility contracts and pay tax. Apply at a Spanish consulate before you travel, or by appointment at a police station in Spain. Appointments can take weeks, so start early.

Driving licence exchange

Once you're resident, many foreign licences need exchanging for a Spanish one within a set period, after which they're no longer valid for driving here. Whether you can swap directly or need to take a Spanish test depends on the country that issued your licence, so check the current rules for yours well before you move.

Pet transport

Dogs, cats and ferrets need a microchip, a rabies vaccination and either an EU pet passport or an animal health certificate, issued within the required time before travel. Allow several weeks, and book a pet-friendly ferry, flight or specialist pet transport company in good time.

Spanish lessons

Even a little Spanish makes a big difference at the town hall, the doctor's and the market. Many towns run low-cost classes for residents, and there are private tutors and language schools along the coast. In Valencian-speaking towns you'll also see Valencian on signs and official letters.

Wills and inheritance

Spanish inheritance rules differ from those in many other countries, and inheritance tax depends on the region and how closely the heirs are related to you. Most owners make a Spanish will covering their Spanish assets, and many can choose the law of their nationality to apply. Take advice from a lawyer who understands both countries.

Currency exchange

If your money is in pounds or another currency, the exchange rate can change the real cost of your home by thousands. A specialist currency provider usually beats a high-street bank, and can fix a rate in advance for your completion date or set up regular transfers for pensions and income.

Utilities

Electricity, water, gas and internet are usually transferred into your name on completion, using your NIE and Spanish bank account. Take meter readings on handover, and check that bills are paid up to date so no debts follow the property. Fibre broadband is widely available, but check coverage before you buy in the hills.

Visa rules, income thresholds and the practical requirements above change. Always confirm the current requirements with an immigration lawyer or your Spanish consulate before you plan around them. We can introduce you to specialists we trust.
